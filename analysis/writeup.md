# Who's mad at AI, and who isn't, on TikTok and YouTube

*Pairie Koh · 2026-05-24*

We scraped AI-related videos from TikTok and YouTube via Bright Data using parallel keyword lists (one for AI backlash, one for AI positive sentiment), classified each one with Claude Sonnet for sentiment and theme, and then re-classified the low-confidence cases with a manual audit. To avoid a recency-skew problem in the scrape, this write-up restricts everything to videos posted in 2026.

---

## AI Backlash

We found 3,668 videos for AI backlash across TikTok and YouTube in 2026 with five themes accounting for more than ~80% of all AI backlash content: jobs/displacement, deepfakes/misinformation, art/creative theft, environment/energy and general anti-AI sentiment. There is a clear difference in content across platforms — TikTok AI backlash content is art-dominant (29% of TikTok backlash) and is driven by the #noAI and #stopAIart artist community. YouTube is jobs- and deepfake-led, with jobs (24%) and deepfakes (20%) together accounting for nearly half of YouTube backlash. Data centers are an interesting cross-cutting hook on YouTube specifically — 23% of all YouTube backlash videos explicitly mention a data center, server farm or GPU farm, more than double the share that get primarily classified as environment content. Interestingly, we found that existential-risk content is only 4.3% of the total content. In terms of engagement, general anti-AI content reaches the broadest audience across both platforms, while existential-risk videos punch above their weight on a per-video basis. The key caveat is that our scraping algorithm skews towards recent posts because the search algorithms surface engagement-recent content first; restricting to 2026 mitigates but does not fully remove this.

## AI Positive Sentiment

We found 7,099 videos for AI positive sentiment across TikTok and YouTube in 2026 — roughly twice the volume of the backlash corpus — with four themes accounting for more than ~83% of all positive content: career and productivity wins (vibe coding, agent demos, ChatGPT productivity), creative-tool use (Midjourney, Suno, Sora), synthetic-media play (funny AI videos, lip sync, animation), and education and learning. There is a clear difference in content across platforms. TikTok positive content is more balanced across career (28%), synthetic-media play (22%) and creative tools (18%), and carries a notable AI-companion slice (AI girlfriend, character.ai, "Claude is my therapist") at 8.8%. While YouTube is dominated by career-productivity (37%) and creative-tool (34%) content together making up over two-thirds of the corpus. Interestingly, we found that breakthrough-science content (medical AI, scientific AI wins) is only 2.1% of total positive content — the rough mirror of existential-risk's 4.3% share on the backlash side, meaning both edges of the "official" AI discourse (lab-CEO uplift and safety-researcher doom) are barely visible on the platforms where the public actually is. In terms of engagement, synthetic-media play reaches the broadest audience on TikTok while creative-tool and career-productivity content dominate reach on YouTube. The key caveat is that the positive corpus is heavily promotional — affiliate marketing, course funnels, sponsored AI tool content — so any "AI is well-loved" framing should be restricted to the genuine-sentiment subset before publication.

## Implications

The most striking pattern across the two corpora is the symmetry of what is missing. Existential risk is 4.3% of the backlash and breakthrough science is 2.1% of the positive — both of the stories that dominate elite AI conversation, the doom story told by safety researchers and the salvation story told by lab CEOs, are nearly invisible at the level of platform content, where the loud themes are jobs, art, deepfakes and a constant stream of "let me show you my new ChatGPT workflow." This has a direct implication for anyone whose communications strategy is built around medical-breakthrough decks or alignment-research talking points: the audience for that framing is real but small, and the constituency for AI policy at the platform level is labor, creative industries and environmental — not safety-research. Data centers are worth singling out as the most operationally significant finding here, because they are physical, photographable, NIMBY-able and they connect otherwise disconnected grievances (jobs, environment, local politics) into a single concrete object with addresses and permits and neighbors; expect data-center NIMBY organizing to become the most consequential form of AI opposition over the next 18 months. On the positive side, the TikTok AI-companion slice at 8.8% deserves more attention than it currently gets in product strategy and mental-health policy circles — it is a use case the labs are uncomfortable foregrounding but which is, on the platforms, larger than nearly every category of positive AI content except career-productivity, creative tools and synthetic-media play. Two more patterns worth holding onto: the platforms are not the same audience (artist-led TikTok and labor-led YouTube are two different backlashes that probably need two different responses), and the themes that dominate share-of-voice are not the themes that dominate per-video reach — existential-risk and synthetic-media-play content are small in count but each video travels much further than a typical jobs rant or productivity tutorial, meaning the loudest content and the most-traveled content are different things.
