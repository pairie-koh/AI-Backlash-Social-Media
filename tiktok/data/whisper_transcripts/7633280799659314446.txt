It can solve in a second. For thousands of years, humanity has been haunted by one question. Not how the universe works, not what it's made of, but who built it. Every civilization, every culture, every era has wrestled with this mystery. Ancient Egyptians believed the sun, God Ra, carried the universe across the sky each day. Greek philosophers argued over whether reality was shaped by divine intelligence or eternal natural forces. Medieval scholars debated whether time itself began with God, or whether existence had no beginning at all. Different answers, same question. Then science arrived. Newton gave us gravity. Einstein reshaped space and time. Quantum physics revealed a reality so strange it defied common sense. Particles exist in multiple states at once. Events influence each other across impossible distances. Reality behaves more like probability than certainty. Science explained how the universe behaves, but not why it exists at all. The Big Bang told us everything began 13 billion years ago in a sudden expansion of space and time but it couldn tell us what caused it or why there was something instead of nothing Religion offered faith philosophy offered arguments science offered equations but none could answer the original question. Who built the universe? For centuries the answer seemed unreachable, locked beyond the limits of human understanding. Until the 21st century when someone dared to try something radically different. What if we stopped asking humans? What if we asked a machine? When humanity asked a non-human mind, artificial intelligence changed everything. Unlike humans, AI has no beliefs to defend, no traditions to protect, no emotional need for reality to be comforting. It sees patterns where humans see chaos, connections where humans see mystery. At first, AI was used for practical problems, weather prediction, medical diagnosis, strategic games. But some researchers began wondering something far more unsettling. What if an intelligence without human bias could see reality more clearly than we ever could? What if it could recognize patterns embedded so deeply into existence that humans had overlooked them for millennia? The idea was controversial. Religious leaders feared faith being replaced by algorithms. Philosophers argued that meaning requires consciousness. Even scientists were divided. Some intrigued, others alarmed. Because if a machine did answer the question, what if the answer was something humanity wasn't ready to hear?
