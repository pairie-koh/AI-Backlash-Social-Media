Let me give you an example. Say you spent weeks building a rag system. You tested on dozens of files. These are clean files, PDFs in Excel. Then a customer tries it. So it follows the prompt and answers anyways. And it answers very confidently. But to the customer, it looks completely wrong. And that's the problem. Normal software fails in ways you can trace it. For example, something breaks, you can get a log that helps you pinpoint the exact problem. With AI agents, you can have logs too. But the logs show you what went in and what came out. They don't tell you why it filled in a gap with something that sounded right, but wasn't. And two days later, when a customer flags it, you're staring at a log that looks completely normal. And that's what makes this different from other engineering problems. It's a decision the model makes based on your prompts. And even with perfect prompts, it decides which chunk is the most relevant. It decides when it has enough context to answer and how to interpret an ambiguous question. And all that shows up in the output and by then, it's already too late. So in demos, it looks like a million-dollar application, but in production with actual data, it looks broken.
