From your smartphone to algorithms on Netflix and Amazon, artificial intelligence is becoming part of daily life. And now, AI is being used for your daily weather forecasts. Environment and Climate Change Canada recently announced it will use artificial intelligence in its weather forecasts beginning this spring. The department is launching a new hybrid model, part AI and part traditional physics-based model, which will be better able to predict major systems like winter storms, heat waves, and atmospheric rivers. While ECC is using the hybrid approach, other government weather agencies and private companies have jumped in with their own dedicated AI models. The European Centre for Medium-Range Forecasts launched its artificial intelligence forecasting system in early 2025. And for some variables, such as near-surface temperatures and mid-level atmospheric conditions, it's outperforming the traditional physics-based model by up to 20%. Meanwhile, Google's GraphCast can generate a highly accurate 10-day global forecast in under one minute. By using decades of historical weather data, these AI-driven models can make huge improvements to the forecast. But they do have their limits, especially with local variables like wind speeds near specific mountain ranges. While the advancements in forecast accuracy are a great step, we still have a long way to go. I use at least a dozen different forecast models right now, and I see on a daily basis just how different the projections can be, whether it's predicting the track of an incoming storm or timing out how quickly the low-level clouds and fog will retreat back to the coast in the early summer. At the end of the day, predicting the future is tough when you have as many moving variables as the atmosphere does, especially here in the Maritimes. Ryan Snodden, CBC News, Halifax. Thank you.
