Earlier this year, the EU told Google if your own AI assistant gets privileged access to the core features of Android, the microphone, the screen, the chips that run AI directly on the device, then any competing AI must get the same access. By July, we will know exactly what that means in practice. The intention is good. More competition, more choice, better products. Nobody disagrees with that goal. The question is whether regulation is the right tool to get there, and whether this particular regulation is going to produce the outcomes it promises. Here is what is actually happening. Right now, Google decides which apps get access to the deepest features of Android. That vetting process exists for a reason. It is part of what keeps your phone secure. Before an app can listen to your microphone or read what is on your screen, Google checks whether it is trustworthy enough to do that. The EU is telling Google that it cannot do that anymore. Any AI service that qualifies under EU rules is legally entitled to get the same access as Google's own AI. And if Google tries to restrict that on security grounds, it risks a fine. So we have replaced a market-based vetting mechanism where Google had strong incentives to keep the platform secure because its reputation depended on it, with a regulatory mandate that removes that incentive entirely. The security check is not improved. It is abolished. Markets are not perfect, but they have one thing regulations often lack, skin in the game. Google has every reason to want Android to be secure. A regulation that removes this ability to act on that incentive, it's not solving a market failure. It is creating one. There is a better path though. If regulation is needed, make it conditional.
