Data centers don't need to use a lot of water. They do because we chose the laziest cooling design and then scaled it across a trillion-dollar industry. See, AI servers generate a lot of heat. In order to get rid of that heat, most facilities use evaporative cooling. That means that water is sprayed and allowed to evaporate to reduce the heat. A typical hyperscale data center can consume over 200 million gallons of water a year just to cool its equipment. That's the equivalent of a town of tens of thousands of people using water for a year. The better solution is liquid-based cooling, or sometimes called direct-to-chip and immersion cooling. Instead of dumping heat into a room in evaporating water, liquid systems move heat directly off the chip with higher efficiency. Because liquid transfers heat thousands of times more effectively than air, these systems can run in closed loops that dramatically decrease water loss, up to 90% according to industry pilots. To make this mainstream, we need three things. First, design construction defaults that assume liquid cooling for all new builds. Two, we need transparency standards around reporting water use and cooling types publicly. Three, we need policy incentives that treat cooling as infrastructure, not as an afterthought. And if we do this, we don't just get lower water use. We get better performance, lower energy consumption, and most importantly, less community conflict.
