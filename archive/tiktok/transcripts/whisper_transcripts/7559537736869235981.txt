Sam Altman, the CEO of OpenAI and unofficial high priest of the AI cult, just randomly admitted that, yeah, this might all be a bubble. He even compared it to the dot-com bubble, that fun time when the Nasdaq lost almost 80% of its value because it turned out most internet companies were just PowerPoints with mascots. And honestly, he might be right. The US stock market has almost doubled in five years, mostly because 10 giant companies promised AI would change literally everything. Altman's raised billions and is already talking about spending trillions on data centers, which is a totally normal thing to say if you're planning to buy a small moon. So here's the real question. If this is a bubble, what did we actually buy? This is where things get weird. Most of the headlines you see about the AI boom aren't about how much value AI has created. They're just about how much the price of a few companies' stocks have gone up, which is kind of like judging the quality of a restaurant by how expensive their forks are. Here's the catch. When a company market value jumps by hundreds of billions it doesn mean that much money magically appeared It just means the last person to buy the stock paid more than the person before them It like if a single banana sells for that doesn mean all the bananas on earth are suddenly worth It just means one very confused person is about to have an expensive smoothie. So if we actually want to know whether this is a bubble, we need to stop looking at stock charts and start counting what's real. The money, the chips, the electricity, the people. Because if you burn through trillions and all you get is a slightly faster email writer, that's not a revolution. That's just really expensive spellcheck with a PR team. Alright, let's talk about what this bubble is actually made of. Because it's not hopes and dreams. It's metal and electricity. And mostly invoices.
