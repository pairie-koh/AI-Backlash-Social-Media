AI art officially can't be copyrighted in the US. The Supreme Court recently declined to hear Thaler v. Paramutter, a case where computer scientist Stephen Thaler tried to copyright an image that his AI system Dabbas made back in 2012. The catch was that he listed the AI as the author and not himself. Every court has now ruled against him, and by refusing to hear this case, the Supreme Court effectively cements that human authorship is a bedrock requirement when it comes to copyright. Even the Trump administration urged them not to take the case. This has pretty big implications. If you're using AI to generate character, artwork, or other creative assets without meaningful human contribution, that work could essentially belong in the public domain. So then anyone could use it and then you have no legal standing to stop them. The ruling also follows Thayer's failed patent case where he tried to list Dabbas as an inventor. Same result there. The patent office said that only natural persons can be listed as inventors. Fowler called the decision substrate chauvinism. Basically, the assumption that creativity must come from a biological brain. But legal experts do point out that this case doesn't actually cover human AI collaborations, just fully autonomous AI creations.
