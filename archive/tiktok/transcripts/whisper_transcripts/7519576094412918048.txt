A new AI data center in the UK could produce more emissions than five Birmingham airports, and not many people seem to be talking about it. Behind every AI interaction, from writing essays to answering messages, is a large-scale data center consuming vast amounts of electricity. These data centers are used to train AI models and run AI searches, services being automatically offered to billions of people by Google and Meta, whether they like it or not. A proposed data center near London is expected to use the same amount of energy as half a million homes, and so much of that power comes from fossil fuels. But it's not just the electricity AR uses. These systems can get really hot, and to stop them melting down, they have to be cooled by water, millions of litres of it, constantly. In 2022, Microsoft alone used over 1.7 billion litres, more than 2,500 Olympic-sized swimming pools, a 34% increase in one single year, enough water for hundreds of thousands of people. In drought-prone areas, AI is literally competing with humans for water. That means asking ChatGPT to write your essay or emails could be using more water than you drink in a single day. As AI spreads faster than regulation, we're being lulled into comfort by the same systems that are burning our planet. But it doesn't have to be this way. Because AI isn't inherently harmful. There is an alternative to sleepwalking into rapidly accelerated climate devastation. Scientists and campaigners have been sharing ways that AI could be made more sustainable and responsible. For example, AI data centers could be powered by renewables and be regulated so they're more transparent, meaning better accountability. AI could help fight deforestation, cut emissions, and design greener cities. It should be supporting our learning and solving global problems like the climate crisis, not deepening it.
