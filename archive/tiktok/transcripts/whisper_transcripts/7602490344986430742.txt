Have you ever heard of fossilised lightning? One second, the sky snaps. Five billion joules punch into sand. And the ground freezes a moment of violence. These are called fulgurites. They form when lightning hits a beach, a desert, even soil, and the heat spikes hotter than the surface of the sun. The sand doesn't melt slowly. It flash-liquifies. Electricity drills downward, carving glass tubes as it goes. Branching, twisting, sometimes hollow, sometimes solid. A lightning bolt you can hold. Some are thin as wires. Others are thicker than your arm. They record the exact path the electricity took, microsecond by microsecond, locked into glass. No two are identical, because no two lightning strikes ever follow the same chaos. You're not looking at a rock. You're looking at a frozen explosion. A storm turned solid And here's the unsettling part That bolt lasted less than a blink But the scar it left can survive for millions of years The sky hit the earth once And the earth never forgot Long after the storm fades
