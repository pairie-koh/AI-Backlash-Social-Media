One of my first issues with this piece is that it makes a very ostensibly clean, logical jump from AI is capable of generating code to AI will replace everything The bottleneck in a lot of real AI adoption right now is not capability it evaluation This is the critical gap in getting AI to contribute that last mile of value that we want to see in actual enterprise systems, where we need better ways, scalable methodologies to identify whether AI is actually doing the things that we want it to do. The piece describes, for example, a procurement manager threatening to replace a six-figure software contract with an AI-built prototype. And I think that anecdote actually reveals a little bit more about negotiation leverage than a technical reality. Getting from 80% functionality to a real successful production-grade reality across edge cases, getting the results that we want to see in these kinds of systems, that's a lot of the cost and it requires a more mature evaluation layer as an industry, something that we need to invest in over the next several years. Ultimately, what I think that means is that the displacement curve is actually dramatically slower than what the Citrini research piece models And that leads me to sort of the second piece of discourse which is even granting a world where AI does eventually deliver real verifiable, reliable value across knowledge work. This long-run conclusion, I think, is really contrary to what we've seen in the market before. The piece argues that once human intelligence is no longer that scarce input, demand for it will drop. But I think history says the opposite. When you dramatically reduce the cost of a valuable input, you explosively expand the frontier of what's actually economically feasible. Cetrini's model treats the demand for knowledge work as fixed, but in reality, demand is elastic. Instead of thinking about this as demand destruction over time, we can think about this in terms of creative destruction, where new jobs are ultimately created and will replace at an even accelerating pace any potential old jobs that were destroyed As a sort of closing note I think that the Citrini piece is definitely useful as a sort of stress test The market is shifting away from pricing AI as pure upside to this existential threat, you know, swinging constantly this pendulum between euphoria and panic with very little of what seems to be analytical rigor in between.
